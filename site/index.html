Multi-task learning · domain shift · EuroSAT → AID · 10 seeds

Does a reconstruction task make a satellite classifier robust to a new sensor?

A course project claimed that training one encoder to classify and reconstruct satellite images makes it generalise better. Re-run over 10 seeds and scored on aerial images from a different sensor, the claim does not hold: hard-sharing MTL lands where a single-task CNN already was, and the gains that do appear follow the input masking, not the reconstruction.

The question

Train on Sentinel-2 patches of forest and residential land (EuroSAT), deploy on Google Earth aerial images of the same two land uses (AID). Classification-only training can latch onto shortcuts of the training sensor; a reconstruction head forces the shared encoder to keep the whole image. Does that auxiliary task buy robustness to the sensor change? The answer decides whether an auxiliary loss is a cheap robustness fix or a distraction from augmentation and target-domain labels.

Setup

  • Train / validate: 6,000 EuroSAT RGB patches (3,000 forest, 3,000 residential, 64×64), stratified 80/20 split drawn per seed.
  • Out of distribution: 120 AID images resized to 64×64: 60 forest, 30 dense residential, 30 medium residential. Never used for training or checkpoint selection.
  • Models (the course notebooks' architectures): a 24k-parameter single-task CNN; a 193k-parameter hard-sharing network (shared encoder, classification head, decoder) trained on α·CE + (1 − α)·MSE; a 360k-parameter soft-sharing network (two branches tied by an alignment loss, masked-autoencoder reconstruction on inputs with 30% of pixels zeroed).
  • Controls: the hard-sharing network with α = 1 (same architecture, reconstruction switched off) separates the auxiliary loss from the larger model; the single-task CNN trained on the same 30%-masked inputs separates soft sharing from its input masking.
  • Protocol: 10 epochs of Adam (lr 10⁻³, batch 32), checkpoint with the lowest validation loss, 10 seeds for the headline variants and 5 for the rest of the α sweep. Differences are paired by seed (same split) with a t interval.

The reconstruction loss does move AID accuracy when the architecture is held fixed: +3.8 pp [+0.7, +6.9] (p = 0.02). But the larger architecture on its own costs -3.2 pp [-5.8, -0.5] (p = 0.02) against the small single-task CNN, so the net result of the course project's comparison, hard-sharing MTL against the CNN, is +0.7 pp [-2.9, +4.2] (p = 0.68). On ranking quality MTL is worse: AUROC -0.053 [-0.086, -0.020] (p = 0.005).

Where the errors are

The single-task CNN gets every AID residential group at least 98% right but only 40% of AID forests. Hard-sharing MTL recovers forests (55%) and pays for them on medium-density residential scenes (77%): it trades one error for another rather than seeing the new sensor better. AUROC (0.82 CNN, 0.77 MTL) is well above what the accuracy suggests: many forests get a lower residential score than the residential images, but still above 0.5.

At 64×64, a Sentinel-2 forest is a smooth green field; a 600×600 Google Earth forest, shrunk to the same size, shows crowns, shadows and clearings. Edge strength (mean grey-level step between neighbouring pixels): EuroSAT forest 1.9, AID forest 7.1, EuroSAT residential 11.1, AID residential 11.7. On this texture measure an AID forest sits between the two training classes.

A single run cannot settle it

AID accuracy after every epoch, one line per seed, while EuroSAT validation accuracy sits near 100%.

Across epochs 3 to 10 of a typical run, AID accuracy spans 16 points (CNN) and 15 points (MTL) while validation accuracy barely moves, so the checkpoint rule cannot see it. Across seeds, the CNN ranges 65%–73% on AID, hard-sharing MTL 63%–78% and soft sharing on clean inputs 50%–74%. The original notebook's single-seed numbers, 73.3% (CNN), 58.3% (MTL) and 50.8% (soft sharing), are draws from these distributions, not a ranking.

Soft sharing: the gain is the masking

The soft-sharing network only ever sees images with 30% of their pixels zeroed. Scored on clean EuroSAT images it reaches 60.7% (99.8% on masked ones) and on clean AID images 57.3% [52.0%, 62.7%]: the notebook's collapse to 50.8% came from scoring a masked-input model on unmasked images. Scored as trained, it reaches 73.3% [69.5%, 77.1%] on AID, +4.0 pp [+0.7, +7.3] above the single-task CNN.

The masking ablation explains that gain. The single-task CNN trained and scored on the same masked inputs reaches 71.9% [70.7%, 73.2%] (+2.6 pp [+0.8, +4.3] against the plain CNN, p = 0.008); soft sharing adds +1.4 pp [-2.3, +5.2] on top (p = 0.41). Its AUROC is higher, 0.89 against 0.81 (+0.078 [+0.056, +0.100], p < 0.001), but with 15× more parameters the comparison cannot credit the reconstruction for it. Used as plain augmentation, masking helps most: the masked-input CNN scored on clean AID images reaches 74.3% [72.9%, 75.8%] (+5.0 pp [+1.9, +8.1] against the plain CNN, p = 0.005; forest 74%), at the price of 94.7% on clean EuroSAT validation.

Limitations

  • The AID set is small: 60 forest images, so one image is 1.7 points of forest accuracy. Confidence intervals are across seeds and do not include the sampling error of the test set itself.
  • 14 paired comparisons are reported, without a multiple-testing correction. At the Bonferroni threshold (p < 0.0036) 3 stay significant: soft sharing vs CNN (AUROC); soft sharing vs masked-input CNN (AUROC); masked-input CNN, clean scoring, vs CNN (AID-forest accuracy). Effects with p between that threshold and 0.05 are suggestive, not established.
  • One architecture family, 10 epochs, no data augmentation beyond the masking ablation, no tuning of α beyond the sweep, the soft-sharing loss weights as set in the notebook.
  • Training ran on an Apple GPU (MPS), which is not bit-for-bit deterministic; re-runs reproduce the distributions, not every digit.
  • Untested here and more likely to help: colour and scale augmentation, a handful of labelled AID images, domain adaptation.
